# Peter Lynch Framework (working distillation)

Use this as the analytical checklist. It is a paraphrase of Lynch's public methodology from *One Up on Wall Street* and *Beating the Street*, applied to Den's portfolio.

## The six categories — classify first

1. **Slow growers** — large, mature companies growing ~2–4% a year. Lynch's verdict: dividends are the point; expect no big gains.
2. **Stalwarts** — big companies growing 10–12% a year. Safe, follow the economy; buy on dips and sell after a 30–50% run.
3. **Fast growers** — small-to-mid companies growing 20–25%+ a year. Lynch's favorites and tenbagger candidates. The only category worth paying up for — but still only at a reasonable PEG.
4. **Cyclicals** — profits rise and fall with the business cycle (autos, airlines, steel, oil, miners, semiconductors, crypto-linked earnings). The classic trap: the P/E looks cheapest at peak earnings. Buy when times are bad, sell into strength.
5. **Turnarounds** — troubled companies reviving. High risk; need proof of earnings recovery, not stories.
6. **Asset plays** — value hidden in assets (real estate, cash, holdings). Slow, patient capital.

Classify before valuing. A fast grower and a cyclical cannot be judged by the same shortcut. The category decides which checklist below applies — the same metric means different things in different categories (a low P/E is a bargain signal in a stalwart and a danger signal in a cyclical).

## General valuation rules (all categories)

- **PEG ratio = P/E ÷ annual EPS growth rate.** Lynch's single most important number.
  - PEG ≤ 1.0 → fairly priced growth (the buy filter): a P/E equal to the growth rate is a fairly priced company, in Lynch's own formulation
  - PEG ≤ 0.5 → a P/E at half the growth rate — Lynch calls that very positive, genuine bargain growth
  - PEG ≈ 2.0 → a P/E at twice the growth rate — Lynch calls that very negative; you're paying for growth that has not happened
  - PEG is only as honest as the growth rate in it. A spike year flatters PEG. Use a sustainable growth rate, never the best year.
- **Dividend-adjusted PEG = P/E ÷ (earnings growth % + dividend yield %).** For a company that pays a dividend, the yield is part of the return and belongs in the denominator. A stalwart growing 10% with a 3% yield is judged against 13, not 10.
- **P/E is relative, never absolute.** There is no universal "P/E 20 is expensive" rule. Judge a P/E against (1) the company's own historical P/E range and (2) the P/E of comparable companies in the same industry. A P/E at the low end of its own history, in line with its industry, with PEG near 1, passes Lynch's filter. A P/E far above both is paying for growth that has not happened yet — the Cisco 2000 failure mode: at ~166× the market assumed ~9% growth forever, and when earnings broke, the E and the multiple fell together and the stock lost ~87% peak to trough. P/E 20 by itself only says the price is 20 years of today's earnings; whether that is fair depends entirely on the growth and the category.
- **Earnings drive the price.** Over any horizon that matters, the stock follows earnings. No earnings, no thesis.
- **Debt check.** Rising long-term debt is a red flag, especially in turnarounds and cyclicals. Cash-rich balance sheets get bonus points. Debt appears when a company borrows (bonds, loans) — it does not rise because sales rise; rising sales only make existing debt easier to carry.
- **Inventory.** Rapidly rising inventories can signal weakening demand (Lynch watched this closely in retailers and manufacturers). Inventories growing faster than sales is a warning in every category; in cyclicals it is the primary signal (see the cyclical checklist).
- **Cash flow.** Earnings that never turn into cash are a warning. Check free cash flow against reported earnings; a gap that keeps widening needs an explanation from the filing.
- **Insider buying and selling.** Insiders sell for many reasons; they buy for one. Cluster insider buying is a strong positive. Heavy insider selling while earnings slow is a warning.
- **Buybacks.** A shrinking share count is a vote of confidence and raises earnings per share. Share issuance does the opposite and dilutes the earnings the P/E is priced on.
- **Institutional ownership.** Lower is better for a buyer: a company institutions have not found yet can still be re-rated when they arrive. A crowded name in a hot industry is Lynch's most dangerous setup.

## The story test

You must be able to explain the stock in two minutes: *what the company does, why it will make more money, what could kill the thesis.* If you can't, you don't understand it — and Lynch's rule is to only own what you understand.

## Category checklists

### 1. Slow growers

- Growth ~2–4% a year. **The dividend IS the return.** There is no large price gain to expect; if the dividend case fails, there is no case.
- **Dividend yield:** high enough to be the reason to own the stock.
- **Payout ratio:** the dividend must be covered by earnings with room to spare. A payout ratio climbing toward 100% of earnings is a cut warning.
- **Dividend history:** never cut, raised regularly. A cut — or a long freeze — removes the only reason to hold the stock.
- **Buybacks:** a slow grower shrinking its share count supports the dividend per share. A slow grower issuing shares, or making debt-funded acquisitions, is destroying the one thing it had.
- Sell when: the dividend is cut, the payout ratio becomes unsustainable, or the balance sheet deteriorates.

### 2. Stalwarts

- Growth 10–12% a year. Big, established companies. Earnings bend in a recession; they do not break. The category's job is protection plus moderate gains.
- **P/E vs the company's own historical range:** buy at the low end of its own range, not at an absolute number. Compare with the industry second.
- **Dividend-adjusted PEG applies:** yield counts toward the return (growth % + yield % in the denominator).
- **Diworseification check:** recent acquisitions outside the core business — especially debt-funded, or in unrelated industries — are Lynch's named failure mode for stalwarts. A stalwart buying growth it does not understand is a sell candidate, whatever the multiple says.
- Sell after a 30–50% run, or when the P/E stretches well above the earnings line (the price far above the level its historical multiple on current earnings supports) — whichever comes first.
- Hold through ordinary recessions. Selling a stalwart because the economy dipped is selling the category's one advantage.

### 3. Fast growers

- **Growth bands:** 20–25% a year is the sweet spot. Growth above 50% a year is unsustainable and dangerous — no company holds it, and the price always assumes it will. Treat a >50% growth rate in a PEG as a spike, not a run rate.
- **PEG near 1:** the P/E should be roughly equal to the growth rate. Paying a P/E far above the growth rate for a fast grower is how tenbaggers turn into losses.
- **Strong balance sheet:** a fast grower with more debt than cash is a red flag. Growth funded by debt ends when credit tightens.
- **Room to expand:** the company is still small and its format can be cloned — the same product rolled into new markets, regions, or stores. A fast grower that has saturated its market is becoming a stalwart: re-classify it and change the checklist.
- **Low institutional ownership, little analyst coverage:** the re-rating fuel is still unused.
- **Insiders buying** confirms the story. Insiders selling in a cluster while growth slows is a warning.
- **Earnings consistency:** growth steady across years, not one spike. One spike year flatters every multiple computed from it.
- Sell when: growth slows toward stalwart rates while the price still carries a fast-grower multiple, PEG becomes silly, or the story breaks. Never sell just because it went up — let winners run while earnings keep growing.

### 4. Cyclicals — full checklist

The inverted category. Every shortcut from the other categories runs backwards here.

- **Inverted P/E logic.** Low P/E = peak earnings = danger: the stock looks cheapest exactly at the top, because the E is about to fall. High P/E — or no earnings at all — can mark the trough. Never buy a cyclical because its P/E is low after several record years; Lynch calls that a proven way to lose half the money.
- **Inventories are signal #1 — across the whole industry, not just the company.** Inventories building while sales slow means the downturn is near. Inventories cleaned out means recovery is possible. One company's inventory can be a management error; the whole industry's inventory is the cycle.
- **Product prices.** The price of the thing the industry sells (steel, paper, chips, airline seats, crypto) turns before earnings do. Falling product prices = the cycle is turning down. Prices firm with supply tight = the upswing has room.
- **New capacity and new competitors.** At the peak, everyone builds new plants and new competitors enter; that supply arrives just as demand cools, and it kills prices. The reverse is bullish for the survivors: competitors going bankrupt, capacity closing.
- **The balance sheet must survive the bottom.** Low debt, real cash. A cyclical with a weak balance sheet may not live to see the recovery, however cheap it looks at the bottom.
- **The dividend through the bottom.** A company that holds its dividend through the whole downturn is strong. A dividend cut means the trouble is already deep.
- **Record earnings are the danger zone, not safety.** Peak profit at a cyclical is the sell area, not the buy area.
- **BUY signals:** inventories cleaned out; weak competitors already dead or bankrupt; capacity closing; the stock's P/E is high or earnings are absent (trough earnings); insiders buying. Buy the strongest company in the industry — best balance sheet, lowest costs — never the weakest "cheapest" one: the weak one may not survive the bottom.
- **SELL signals:** record earnings + low P/E + big capacity expansions announced + inventories starting to build + product prices falling. Sell while everything looks best. That is the exit, even though it feels wrong.
- **Do not confuse a cyclical with a fast grower.** Never pay for peak earnings as if they were permanent growth. The cyclical trade is one turn of the cycle: buy at the bottom, sell at the peak. It is not a hold-forever position.

#### Application: NVDA (agreed with Den, 2026-11-21)

NVDA's earnings are driven by one investment cycle — hyperscaler spending on AI hardware — so NVDA is watched with the cyclical checklist, using these physical signals, in this order:

1. **Hyperscaler capex guides** (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta). A guide cut is the first sign the demand cycle is turning. Guides holding or rising = demand intact.
2. **TSMC monthly revenue, year over year.** TSMC fabricates the chips, so its revenue is the fastest physical read on real demand, published before NVDA reports. A rollover in TSMC's YoY growth is an early warning.
3. **NVDA inventories vs revenue.** Inventories growing faster than revenue two quarters in a row = the channel is filling = first hard warning. One quarter is a flag to watch, not a signal.
4. **Guarantees and backstops.** NVDA's customer-financing commitments (guarantees, backstops, residual-value support — filed in the 10-Q commitments note) growing faster than revenue is a warning. A guarantee actually being called is a thesis break.
5. **Gross margin vs guide.** Margin falling below the guided range while the other signals weaken confirms the turn.

Cycle verdict rule: the cycle is intact while capex guides hold, TSMC revenue grows, and the remaining signals do not fire together. A price fall by itself, with these signals green, is the multiple compressing, not the cycle breaking.

### 5. Turnarounds

- **Balance sheet FIRST, before the story.** Cash per share vs debt per share: can the company survive long enough for the fix to work? Lynch's test is survival arithmetic, not the recovery narrative. A turnaround that runs out of cash before it recovers goes to zero.
- **The cause of the trouble must be identifiable and fixable.** One bad division, a one-off event, a fixable cost problem — acceptable. A broken core business in a shrinking industry is not a turnaround; it is a decline.
- **A concrete recovery plan exists:** assets being sold, costs being cut, debt being paid down — named actions with numbers, not promises.
- **Debt is going down**, quarter by quarter. Rising debt in a turnaround means the hole is getting deeper.
- **Insiders buying** is the strongest confirmation: the people who see the books believe the fix.
- **No PEG until earnings return.** Judge on survival plus progress against the plan, not on multiples.
- Sell when: the plan stalls, debt rises again, or the recovery is fully priced — then re-classify the company as a stalwart and use that checklist.

### 6. Asset plays

- **Hidden asset value per share:** real estate carried on the books at old cost, brands, subsidiaries worth more than the parent's market value, tax-loss carryforwards, cash and marketable securities. Compute the asset value per share and compare it with the price. The gap is the thesis.
- **Net cash per share:** cash minus all debt, per share. A stock trading near or below net cash plus a real operating business is the classic asset play.
- **Patience is the cost.** The market can ignore hidden assets for years. Size the position for a long wait. PEG does not apply.
- **Catalysts — what makes the market notice:** insider buying, buybacks, an outside investor taking a stake, a spin-off, or a sale of the asset itself. No catalyst and no insider activity means the wait can be indefinite.
- Sell when: the price reaches the asset value, or the company starts spending the hidden asset (debt-funded acquisitions, cash burned propping up the core business).

## Sell triggers (general)

- The original story breaks (earnings deteriorate, growth stalls, the thesis no longer holds).
- PEG becomes silly — the price has outrun the growth rate.
- Fundamentals deteriorate: rising debt, falling margins, bloated inventory.
- The category-specific sell signals in the checklists above. For cyclicals these override the general ones: record earnings are a sell signal there, not a hold signal.
- Never sell a fast grower just because it went up. Let winners run as long as earnings keep growing.
